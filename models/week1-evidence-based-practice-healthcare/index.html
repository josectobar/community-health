Chapter 2 · Evidence-Based Practice

Identifying Evidence-Based Sources in Health Promotion

Learn the major EBP registries, then test your ability to tell evidence-based sources from non-evidence-based ones.

Evidence-based practice means using the best available research evidence — combined with professional judgment and community values — to design health promotion programs. Several established registries and databases have done the systematic review work for you. Click each source to learn more.

Not all sources are equal. A vendor brochure, a testimonial, or a single unpublished study is not evidence-based in the same sense as a systematic review or a federally vetted program registry.
COCHRANE

Cochrane Reviews

International systematic review network

✓ Evidence-Based ▾ expand

Cochrane is an international network of researchers who produce systematic reviews of healthcare and public health interventions. Cochrane reviews are considered among the highest standards of evidence in the world.

  • Reviews synthesize results from multiple high-quality studies
  • Independent — no commercial funding influences reviews
  • Covers public health, behavioral health, clinical care, and health promotion
  • Freely searchable at cochrane.org

Why use it: When writing a program rationale, citing a Cochrane review that supports your proposed intervention is one of the strongest evidence claims you can make.

NCI
RTIPs

NCI Research-Tested Intervention Programs (RTIPs)

National Cancer Institute registry

✓ Evidence-Based ▾ expand

The National Cancer Institute's RTIPs database compiles cancer control interventions that have been tested in research studies and rated for scientific rigor. Programs are organized by cancer type, target population, and setting.

  • Programs reviewed and rated by NCI scientists
  • Includes evidence summaries and implementation materials
  • Particularly useful for cancer screening, tobacco cessation, and nutrition programs

Why use it: If your program targets cancer-related health issues, RTIPs lets you identify and adapt an existing, tested intervention — saving time and strengthening your rationale.

SAMHSA

SAMHSA Evidence-Based Practices Resource Center

Substance Abuse & Mental Health Services Administration

✓ Evidence-Based ▾ expand

SAMHSA's resource center compiles evidence-based practices for mental health and substance use prevention and treatment. It integrates the former National Registry of Evidence-based Programs and Practices (NREPP).

  • Programs reviewed and rated for quality of research and readiness for dissemination
  • Covers substance use prevention, mental health promotion, and treatment
  • Includes implementation tools and training resources

Why use it: Essential for programs addressing mental health, substance use, and behavioral health. The registry tells you what works and how strongly the evidence supports it.

THE
COMM.
GUIDE

The Community Guide (Community Preventive Services Task Force)

U.S. systematic review repository for public health

✓ Evidence-Based ▾ expand

The Community Guide is a free, evidence-based resource developed by the Community Preventive Services Task Force. It summarizes scientific evidence for community preventive services, policies, and programs across many health topics.

  • Systematic reviews across cardiovascular disease, cancer, diabetes, tobacco, obesity, and more
  • Clear recommendations based on strength of evidence
  • Referenced in the program rationale chapter as a key source (e.g., "evidence of effectiveness — e.g., The Community Guide")

Why use it: Directly referenced in your textbook. When a rationale cites a Community Guide recommendation, it carries significant weight with public health decision makers.

CDC
WONDER

CDC WONDER / National Center for Health Statistics

Epidemiological data source (Step 1 — Background Info)

✓ Evidence-Based ▾ expand

The CDC's WONDER database and the National Center for Health Statistics provide the epidemiological data essential to Step 1 of the rationale — establishing the status and severity of a health problem in a given population.

  • Mortality, morbidity, incidence, and prevalence data by geography and population
  • Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) data
  • National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) data

Why use it: This is your primary source for establishing the scope of the problem in your rationale. It gives you the numbers you will translate through social math into compelling, locally relevant language.
