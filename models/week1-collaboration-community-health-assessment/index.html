Collaboration in Community Health Assessment

A community health assessment requires bringing the right people together. Three distinct groups must be identified and engaged from the start:

Interprofessional team

Other health and public health professionals whose expertise complements yours — nurses, social workers, epidemiologists, physicians.

Stakeholders

Individuals and organizations with a direct interest in health outcomes — residents, schools, faith communities, and local nonprofits.

Decision-makers

People who can act on findings, allocate funding, or implement policy — directors, administrators, and elected officials.

As a health education specialist, you are not just a content expert — you are a connector and coordinator. Collaboration is a core professional competency, not an optional add-on.

Different groups bring different priorities, vocabularies, and pressures. These differences are normal — but they require intentional navigation.

Physician

Focuses on clinical outcomes and medical evidence

Community organizer

Prioritizes resident trust and lived experience

Government official

Driven by budget constraints and policy timelines

Your role as a health education specialist

  • Identify where priorities align across groups
  • Facilitate open communication between groups
  • Keep the focus on the community's needs — not any one profession's agenda

Health promotion is grounded in five foundational principles. These explain why health education specialists cannot rely on intuition or one-size-fits-all programs. Click why this matters under each one.

  • 1

    Health status can be changed.

    This is the foundation of the entire field. If health couldn't change, there would be no reason for health education to exist.

  • 2

    Behavior can be changed, and those changes can influence health.

    This justifies behavioral interventions. It means programs we design can actually move the needle on health outcomes.

  • 3

    Initiating and maintaining behavior change is complex and difficult.

    This warns against simplistic "just tell them what to do" approaches. Programs must account for barriers, stages of readiness, and the possibility of relapse.

  • 4

    Before behaviors can change, the determinants, nature, and motivation for the behavior must be understood. Individuals must be ready to change.

    This is why assessment always comes before intervention. You can't design an effective program without understanding what's actually driving the behavior.

  • 5

    Health is multidimensional — shaped by individual behavior, social factors, biology, health services, and policymaking interacting together.

    No single-factor solution works. Effective programs must be tailored to address the full web of influences on a specific community's health.
