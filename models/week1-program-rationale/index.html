Steps in Creating a Program Rationale

Explore the steps, test your knowledge, and practice applying the concepts.

A program rationale follows an inverted triangle structure — starting broad (national/global data) and narrowing to your specific population and proposed solution. Click each step to explore it.

STEP 1 — BROADEST

Identify Appropriate Background Information

Start with a literature review. Gather evidence from multiple sources to build the foundation of your argument. The data you collect must address several questions:

  • What is the current status of the health problem in this population?
  • What do community members themselves need and want (consumer research)?
  • How does the local problem connect to state and national trends?
  • Is there evidence that programs like this one actually work?
  • What is the potential return on investment — economic, human, or organizational?

Key data sources: Epidemiological data, economic data (CBA, CEA, ROI), evidence of effectiveness (e.g., The Community Guide), and consumer research data.

STEP 2

Title the Rationale

Give the rationale a clear, professional title using this format:

"A Rationale for [Title of Program]: A Program to Enhance the Health of [Name of Priority Population]"

The title is not cosmetic — it signals to decision makers exactly what you are proposing and for whom. A specific, professional title sets the tone for everything that follows.

STEP 3 — CORE

Write the Content of the Rationale

This is where the argument is made. Follow this sequence:

  • Open with the big picture — establish the problem at a global or national level first.
  • Narrow to local — show how the local problem reflects the larger one.
  • Create the problem statement — define what the problem is, why it matters, why it must be addressed, and what the data shows.
  • Use social math — translate statistics into language that resonates with your audience (e.g., "47 community members will die from this this year" vs. a rate per 100,000).
  • Propose the solution — describe the program and what it will accomplish.
  • State why it will work — cite evidence of effectiveness for programs like yours.
STEP 4 — NARROWEST

List the References

Provide a complete reference list for all sources cited. This is not a formality — it signals to decision makers that the rationale is grounded in the best available evidence, not in assumptions or enthusiasm.

A strong reference list protects the program: if questions arise about the evidence base, the sources are there to examine. It also demonstrates professional rigor and credibility.

What is a Program Rationale?

A program rationale is a written justification used to gain the support of decision makers. It is not a program plan or a list of activities — it is a documented, evidence-backed argument that a health problem exists, that it matters to this community, and that the proposed program can address it.

Decision Makers vs. Stakeholders

Decision Makers

Individuals in authority to make a full range of decisions about health promotion. They control resources — funding, staff, access, infrastructure. The rationale is primarily written for them.

Stakeholders

Any person or organization with a vested interest in a program. They matter — but may not hold final authority. Their perspectives should inform the rationale without replacing the decision-maker focus.

The Inverted Triangle Structure

The rationale moves from broad to narrow — like an inverted triangle. You begin at the national or global level, move to state and regional data, then narrow to your specific community, population, and proposed program.

This structure works because it contextualizes the local problem within a larger, credible body of evidence before asking decision makers to act on it.

Social Math — Why It Matters

Raw statistics rarely persuade. Social math is the practice of translating data into language that is meaningful and resonant to your specific audience.

Without Social Math

"The mortality rate is 12.3 per 100,000 population."

With Social Math

"47 people in this county will die from this condition this year."

What Makes a Strong Rationale?

According to the textbook, the rationale should:

1

Show how the benefits of the program align with the values of decision makers

2

Address the potential return on investment (economic, organizational, human)

3

Be backed by the best evidence available — not assumption or anecdote
