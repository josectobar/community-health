The TTM is a stage theory — its core framework is the stages of change. Interventions must match the participant's current stage. The model also includes processes of change, decisional balance, self-efficacy, and temptation.

SR Theory organizes behavioral consequences into a 2×2 framework. Reinforcement always increases behavior; punishment always decreases it. Both can be positive (adding something) or negative (removing something).

This table compares the four theories across key dimensions relevant to program planning. Use it to understand when each theory is most appropriate.

Theory Type Core driver of behavior Key planning question Best suited when...
TPB Continuum · Stage-agnostic Intention — shaped by attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control Does the person intend to act, and does that intention translate to behavior? Behavior is volitional and social norms or perceived control are relevant barriers
HBM Continuum · Threat-based Perceived threat (susceptibility + severity) and net value of action (benefits minus barriers) Does the person feel at risk, and do they think the action is worth it? The behavior is preventive and the population may not perceive themselves as at risk
TTM Stage theory Stage of readiness — and the processes, decisional balance, and self-efficacy relevant at each stage Where is this person in the change process, and what does someone at that stage need? The population has mixed readiness or sustaining long-term change is the goal
SR Continuum · Behaviorist Reinforcement history — the consequences that follow behavior determine its frequency What consequences are currently maintaining this behavior, and what new ones can be introduced? The intervention uses incentives, reward systems, environmental cues, or behavioral contracting

A note on combining theories

These theories are not mutually exclusive. A smoking cessation program might use TTM to stage the audience, HBM to raise perceived susceptibility, TPB to address social norms, and SR principles to structure a reward system. Combining theories thoughtfully addresses more dimensions of behavior change.
