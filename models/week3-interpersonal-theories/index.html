Self-Efficacy

Click to explore

Your belief that you CAN do it.

Self-efficacy is built through four sources: past mastery, observing similar others succeed, verbal encouragement, and reading your own physiological responses.

Program strategy: Start with small wins to build mastery. Use peer testimonials ("She did it — I can too").

Observational Learning

Click to explore

Learning by watching others.

Four conditions must be met: attention (noticing the model), retention (remembering it), reproduction (physical ability), and motivation (desire to replicate).

Program strategy: Use peer role models — people who look like, sound like, and live like your target population.

Outcome Expectations

Click to explore

What you believe will happen if you act.

Expectations can be physical (feel better), social (gain respect), or self-evaluative (feel proud). When expected benefits outweigh costs, behavior is more likely.

Program strategy: Explicitly address myths and correct negative expectations. Highlight positive outcomes from real community members.

Behavioral Capability

Click to explore

Knowledge plus Skills to act.

You can't do something you don't know how to do. Behavioral capability requires both cognitive knowledge ("what to do") and skill mastery ("how to do it").

Program strategy: Provide step-by-step skills training, not just information delivery. Practice beats lecture.

Self-Regulation

Click to explore

Goal-setting and self-monitoring over time.

People sustain behavior change when they set specific goals, track progress, and adjust strategies when they fall short. This is how short-term actions become lasting habits.

Program strategy: Incorporate goal-setting worksheets, progress journals, or apps that allow self-monitoring.

Reciprocal Determinism

Click to explore

Person, Behavior, and Environment — all influence each other.

This is the defining idea of SCT: behavior is not caused by the person alone or the environment alone. Change any piece of the triangle and the others shift.

Program strategy: Intervene at multiple points — change the environment AND build skills AND address attitudes simultaneously.

Social Capital Theory: Sorting Scenarios

Bonding Capital

Within a group — strong ties, shared identity, high trust. Provides emotional support and resources within the in-group.

Bridging Capital

Across groups — connecting people with different backgrounds. Spreads information and norms beyond in-group boundaries.

Linking Capital

To power — connecting community members to institutions or authority figures. Enables advocacy and access to formal resources.

For each scenario, select the type of social capital it represents — Bonding, Bridging, or Linking.
