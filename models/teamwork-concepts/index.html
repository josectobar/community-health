Read the results

Three questions about what the Northwestern team actually found. Write your own answer first, then open the response and compare. Your writing stays in your browser and is not submitted.

1. Length of stay and cost did not improve. Does that mean the intervention failed?

Compare with a strong response
No. The speaker points out that their length of stay was already good, with an observed to expected ratio at or below 1, so there was little room to move. Meanwhile adverse events fell from 7.2 to 3.9 per 100 patient days and nurse ratings of collaboration rose from the mid thirties to 76 percent. Choosing the right measure for what an intervention can actually change is part of evaluating it fairly.

2. Why does the video keep returning to how nurses rate collaboration, rather than how physicians rate it?

Compare with a strong response
Because that is the rating tied to patient outcomes. Higher collaboration reported by nurses was associated with lower risk adjusted mortality. It is also the rating that moved when the interventions worked, while physician ratings were already high and stayed high on control units. A measure that is high before you start cannot tell you whether anything changed.

3. The nice catch conversations stopped three specific errors. What do all three have in common?

Compare with a strong response
In each one, someone other than the physician held the piece of information that mattered: the nurse knew about the contrast reaction, the pharmacist knew the heparin range had changed, the nurse noticed the patient was off telemetry. The information already existed on the unit. What was missing was a reliable time and place for it to be said out loud, which is exactly what structured rounds created.

If you can answer these three in your own words, you are ready to write about interventions and their results in the short paper.
