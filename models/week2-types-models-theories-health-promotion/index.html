Chapter 7 · Theories and Models

Types of Models and Theories in Health Promotion

Explore the framework, understand the levels, and test your knowledge.

These foundational terms are the vocabulary of theoretical work in health promotion. Understanding the distinction between them is essential before selecting or applying any framework.

Theory

A set of interrelated concepts, definitions, and propositions that present a systematic view of a situation — indicating relations among variables to explain and predict events (Kerlinger, 1986).

Model

A composite of ideas and concepts taken from multiple theories and used together (Hayden, 2019). More practical and integrative than a single theory.

Concept

The primary building blocks of a theory (Glanz et al., 2008). General ideas that a theory is organized around.

Construct

A concept used within a specific theory. When a concept is adopted into a particular theory, it becomes a construct of that theory.

Variable

The measurement of a construct. Variables operationalize constructs so they can be studied in research and evaluated in programs.

Key Principle

There is no single "best" theory. The best theory is the one that meets the needs of any given program and priority population.

Theory vs. Model — Why the Distinction Matters

Theory

Explains the why behind behavior. Provides a single explanatory lens grounded in research. Used to understand what drives or prevents behavior change.

Model

Draws on multiple theories. More practical and integrative. Used to organize the planning process or structure how an intervention is designed and delivered.

Planning Models & Theories

These frameworks provide structure for the process of designing, implementing, and evaluating a health promotion program. They answer the question: How do I organize this work?

A planning model does not tell you what drives behavior — it tells you how to go about building a program. Pair it with a behavior change theory to address both process and content.
Planning The Generalized Model ▾

A widely used step-by-step framework for health program planning. Phases include pre-planning, needs assessment, goal and objective setting, intervention development, implementation, and evaluation.

The community needs assessment lives in the first two phases. What planners learn there directly shapes every decision that follows.

Planning PRECEDE-PROCEED Model ▾

A comprehensive planning framework that begins with the desired outcome (health quality of life) and works backward to identify the factors that must be addressed. PRECEDE focuses on assessment; PROCEED guides implementation and evaluation.

Particularly useful for complex, multi-level programs targeting populations with varied health determinants.

Behavior Change Theories

These theories explain the why behind health behavior. They identify what factors drive or prevent behavior change and guide what an intervention needs to address. They divide into two sub-types:

Continuum theories

Identify variables that influence actions and combine them to predict behavior. Change is on a spectrum — the goal is to shift people along it.

Stage theories

Propose an ordered set of categories — people move through stages and different factors drive movement at each stage. Must "stage" the audience first.

Continuum Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) ▾
Intrapersonal

A value-expectancy theory — explains how beliefs and attitudes influence behavior. Built on the earlier Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA). Most useful with volitional behaviors (those under conscious control).

Intention is the core driver, shaped by three factors:

  • Attitude toward the behavior — personal evaluation (good/bad, worth it/not)
  • Subjective norm — perceived social pressure from important others
  • Perceived behavioral control — belief in ability to perform the behavior

Actual behavioral control combined with intention determines performance.

Continuum Health Belief Model (HBM) ▾
Intrapersonal

Another value-expectancy theory. Health-related action depends on the simultaneous occurrence of three classes of factors:

  • Motivation — general health motivation
  • Perceived threat — perceived susceptibility + perceived severity
  • Perceived benefits vs. barriers — will the action help? Is it worth the cost?

Self-efficacy impacts perceived barriers — if a person doesn't believe they can do the behavior, barriers loom larger.

Stage Transtheoretical Model (TTM / Stages of Change) ▾
Intrapersonal

Sometimes called the Stages of Change Model. Helps explain how people move through behavior change over time. People are not uniformly ready — interventions must match the person's current stage.

Core constructs: stages of change, processes of change, decisional balance, self-efficacy, and temptation.

The 5 stages: Precontemplation → Contemplation → Preparation → Action → Maintenance

Key insight: the same intervention will not work for someone in precontemplation and someone in action. Planners must stage the audience first.

Continuum Stimulus Response (SR) Theory ▾
Intrapersonal

A stimulus is presented and a subsequent behavior is performed. Behavior frequency is determined by reinforcements — reinforcement strengthens behaviors; punishment suppresses them.

Reinforcement and punishment can each be positive (adding something) or negative (removing something):

  • Positive reinforcement — add a reward → behavior increases
  • Negative reinforcement — remove an aversive stimulus → behavior increases
  • Positive punishment — add an aversive stimulus → behavior decreases
  • Negative punishment — remove a reward → behavior decreases
Continuum Protection Motivation Theory (PMT) ▾
Intrapersonal

A value-expectancy theory explaining how persuasive communication affects attitudes and behavior through cognitive threat appraisal. Two appraisal processes determine behavior:

  • Threat appraisal — weighs rewards of unhealthy behavior against perceived severity and vulnerability
  • Coping appraisal — assesses response efficacy (will this action work?) and self-efficacy (can I do it?)

The combined outcome of these appraisals determines whether protective motivation occurs.

Continuum Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM) ▾
Intrapersonal

Explains how persuasion messages aimed at attitude change are received. Attitude change can occur through two routes:

  • Central route — high elaboration (deep cognitive processing). Arguments are carefully considered. Attitude change is stronger and more lasting.
  • Peripheral route — low elaboration (shallow processing). Attitude change is influenced by cues (attractiveness, authority) rather than argument quality. Less durable.

Elaboration = the amount of cognitive processing. Many factors affect elaboration (motivation, ability, message relevance).

Continuum Information-Motivation-Behavioral (IMB) Skills Model ▾
Intrapersonal

Three fundamental determinants of preventive behavior:

  • Information — must be relevant and actionable; serves as a personal guide to behavior
  • Motivation — both personal motivation and social motivation are needed to act
  • Behavioral skills — objective ability AND self-efficacy to perform the behavior

All three must be based on the specific needs of the priority population. Missing any one diminishes effectiveness.

Stage Precaution Adoption Process Model (PAPM) ▾
Intrapersonal

A stage model best used for new precautions (e.g., a new vaccination) or abandoning a risky behavior (e.g., stopping a single unsafe habit). Works best for single-step behaviors, not gradual change over time.

Includes seven stages; people can be "staged" through a series of questions.

Key distinction from TTM: PAPM is designed for novel behaviors where people may not even be aware the issue applies to them — Stage 1 is "unaware of the issue," which TTM does not have.

Interpersonal Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) ▾
Interpersonal

Combines SR theory with cognitive theory. Explains behavior through triadic reciprocal causation — internal personal factors, behavioral patterns, and environmental influences all interact bidirectionally.

Key constructs include self-efficacy, reciprocal determinism, behavioral capability, reinforcement (direct, vicarious, self), expectations, expectancies, self-regulation, locus of control, and emotional coping response.

Widely applicable across health topics. Especially strong when both social learning and individual cognition are central to the behavior change goal.

Interpersonal Social Capital Theory ▾
Interpersonal

Focuses on relationships and structures within a community. Social capital is a collective asset — not an individual one. It refers to the trust, reciprocity, and networks that make collective action possible.

Three network types:

  • Bonding — strong ties among similar people
  • Bridging — weak ties across different groups (valuable for new resources)
  • Linking — connections to people/institutions in power
Community Diffusion of Innovations Theory ▾
Community

Explains the spread of innovations (something new) through a population. Useful for designing interventions or messaging for people at different stages of adoption.

Adopter categories: Innovators → Early adopters → Early majority → Late majority → Laggards

Health promotion application: design messages and interventions that match where various segments of the priority population fall in the adoption curve.

The socioecological approach is the overarching concept organizing behavior change theories. It holds that human behavior is shaped by — and shapes — multiple simultaneous levels of influence. Click each level to see what it includes and how it connects to theory selection.

This is the level most theories in Chapter 7 address. Interventions at this level aim to change what a person knows, believes, or feels confident about. Examples: TPB, HBM, TTM, PMT, ELM, IMB, PAPM, SR Theory. Limitation: ignores the social and environmental context that also shapes behavior.
Interpersonal-level theories recognize that we don't change in isolation — our social relationships shape what we do. Examples: Social Cognitive Theory, Social Capital Theory, Social Network Theory. Programs at this level may use peer health educators, social support groups, or family-based interventions.
Workplace wellness policies, school nutrition rules, and hospital protocols are examples of institutional-level influences. Changing organizational culture or policy can have a broader and more lasting impact than individual-level change alone.
Community-level theories include Diffusion of Innovations and the Community Readiness Model. Programs at this level focus on community organizing, norm change, and mobilizing collective action. Assessing community readiness is essential before launching interventions here.
The broadest level of influence. Tobacco taxes, food labeling laws, built environment design (bike lanes, parks), and insurance coverage are all policy-level factors that shape health behavior across entire populations. The most powerful and most difficult to change.

Why this matters for theory selection

A program that only addresses the intrapersonal level may miss the social environment where behavior is reinforced. An interpersonal intervention in a community with deeply entrenched policy barriers may achieve limited reach. Effective health promotion considers which level(s) the evidence points to — and selects theories accordingly.
